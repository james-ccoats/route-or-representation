Project proposal

Route or representation?

How much of the few-shot benefit in a mixture-of-experts model comes from the context redirecting tokens to different experts, and how much from the context reshaping what those experts see?

Question

Two sources of few-shot gain

In a normal MoE layer, each token's hidden state is read by the router, which picks experts and mixing weights. The experts then transform that same hidden state. Few-shot examples in the prompt can change both steps.

Routing change

Attention carries information from the examples into the target tokens' hidden states, so the router can pick different experts or weights. Better-suited experts (for example, ones tuned to the subject or answer format) may then process the question.

Shifted hidden states

The same experts can still do better work: a hidden state enriched by the examples (task format, domain cues) gives each expert a more informative input, so its output moves toward the correct answer.

Both channels start from the same cause, the context's effect on hidden states, and can act together. We expect the gain to come from a combination, and want to isolate each factor and estimate its share.

Related work

Motivation

  • RARE steers MoE behavior by editing hidden states in the router's null space, so routing is unchanged while behavior moves. Demonstrates hidden-state shifts alone can carry behavioral change.
  • Routing Drift Alone Does Not Diagnose Failure In Merged MoE LLMs: closest methodological precedent, and it includes OLMoE. Shows that shifted routing in merged LLMs does not necessarily mean task loss, and that replaying source routing onto merged hidden states changed OLMoE accuracy by no significant amount. The authors do not read this as harmlessness. Releases a routing-analysis toolkit.
  • The Myth of Expert Specialization in MoEs: argues that routing reflects hidden-state geometry, since router logits are a linear function of the hidden state. Motivates our expectation that the representation carries much of the gain. It is observational, so whether routing matters causally for accuracy is untested.
  • Research gap: to our knowledge, none of these applies a routing-replay intervention to ordinary in-context learning. A literature search is still in progress.

Method

Routing replay

  • Gain kept (routing-removed) = (forced − zero-shot) / (few-shot − zero-shot), where forced is the few-shot prompt run with zero-shot routing. High means the gain mostly survives without the target tokens' routing change.
  • Gain added (routing-added) = (forced − zero-shot) / (few-shot − zero-shot), where forced is now the zero-shot prompt run with few-shot routing. Low means the target tokens' routing alone does not reproduce the gain.
  • Full replay forces both the experts and their mixing weights (headline setting). Selection-only forces just which experts are chosen (robustness check).

Routing is replayed one-to-one onto the identical question tokens, matched by character offsets with token ids validated. In these two runs the example tokens keep their own routing, so on their own they do not show that hidden states carry the gain. Slide 9 adds that test.

Method: caveat

Replay mixes two effects

Replay presents the model with a hidden state and a routing decision that were not computed together. Any change in accuracy therefore mixes two effects: the effect of the routing itself, and the effect of this mismatch.

1. Measure the mismatch size

Per layer, how different the routing is between the two prompts on the target tokens (expert-set overlap), and how much of the hidden-state shift lies in directions the router can use. This is the size of the mismatch that replay creates. It does not separate the two effects by itself: the controls do.

2. Matched replay

Overwrite a few-shot prompt's routing on the question tokens with routing from a sibling prompt: same subject as the question, same question, different examples. The context is nearly identical, so accuracy should stay near few-shot. A drop means replay itself is harmful.

  • Prompt A: physics examples (set 1) + question Q
  • Prompt B: physics examples (set 2) + the same Q
  • Run A with B's routing forced on Q's tokens
Method: caveat

Calibrating the damage from replay

Replay is a perturbation with a size. We trace the damage that graded perturbations cause and read our result against it. Positions are illustrative.

  • Graded anchors: matched replay keeps the context benefit, so its loss is mostly damage. Next-best swap and random experts in some layers perturb a prompt's own routing on the question tokens, the same positions replay overwrites, at graded sizes. Random experts in all layers is the extreme. Expert-set overlap with the original routing places each on the axis. Slide 9 applies the same anchors to the example tokens.
  • Below-baseline rule: removing routing should drop accuracy to zero-shot at worst. Clearly below it (a negative gain kept) signals replay damage.
  • Identity check first: forcing a prompt's own routing back onto itself changes nothing.

  • Assuming damage never helps, replay damage only lowers gain kept, so a high gain kept is robust. A low one needs these checks.
  • Results should agree under selection-only and full replay.
  • Matched replay applies to the few-shot prompt only. The self-perturbation anchors apply to either prompt, so routing-added has damage anchors too. Damage also produces a low gain added, so a low value is weak evidence for H4.

The anchors estimate damage at our dose by interpolation. Self-perturbations are incoherent changes, while replay swaps in a coherent alternative, so damage may differ at the same overlap, and may not depend on dose alone.

Method

Replaying the example tokens too

Routing is decided per token, so one forward pass can replay the question tokens and the example tokens separately. The zero-shot prompt has no example tokens, so there is no zero-shot routing to copy onto them. We perturb their routing instead, using the same anchors as before.

Positions

  • Question tokens: routing-removed and routing-added (headline).
  • Example tokens: few-shot prompt only, routing perturbed.
  • Both in one pass.

Perturbations on the examples

  • Next-best swap, or random experts in k of 16 layers (graded).
  • Matched replay: routing from a different example set of equal token length (planned; needs equal-length sets).
  • Random experts in all layers (floor).

What it adds

Gain that survives example-token perturbation, at doses the damage curve says are tolerable, shows routing at the examples is not needed either. With a high gain kept, routing at neither position set carries the gain. It would not identify which non-routing path does.

A drop in gain is read against the damage anchors, as for the question tokens. Perturbing the examples changes the keys and values the question tokens attend to, which is part of the effect and also a source of damage.

Experimental design

Setup

Task and scoring

  • MMLU multiple choice, scored by option log-probs; no generation.
  • Accuracy plus correct-option log-prob (smoother).

Three conditions

  • zero-shot
  • few-shot with examples from the question's own subject (primary)
  • irrelevant examples from another subject, matched on k and token length (control)

Shots and replicates

  • k = 1 to 5, nested.
  • 3 independent example sets, pooled dev + validation.
  • Identical target-question block in every condition.

Subjects

  • Several MMLU science subjects (for example biology, chemistry, physics, computer science), each its own example source and target.
  • Examples never include the question being scored.
  • Cross-domain examples are an extension.

Experimental design

Controls

Identity check: is the machinery sound?

Forcing a prompt's own routing back onto itself must change nothing. Log probs should remain the same within small numerical bounds.

Graded anchors: how much damage does replay do?

Matched replay (routing from a different example set of the same setup) keeps the context benefit, so its loss is mostly damage. Next-best swap and random experts in some layers perturb a prompt's own routing at graded sizes. Their accuracy loss against expert-set overlap traces a damage curve to read replay against.

Random-expert floor: does forcing bite, and how bad can it get?

Random experts are about the worst plausible replacement, so accuracy must drop. That shows the hook changes behavior, and gives a floor for the damage forcing can do.

Irrelevant examples

Separates "any context shifts routing" from "relevant context shifts routing". Also forced like the few-shot prompt (planned), so gain kept and gain added show how much of the split is generic context and length.

Reading order

1. Sanity checks pass. 2. The few-shot gain is clearly nonzero. 3. Only then read gain kept and gain added, with bootstrap intervals, against the damage anchors and the random floor, flagging any result below zero-shot.

Engineering

Reproducible and resumable

Pinned inputs

MMLU is pinned to a dataset commit hash and sampling is seeded. Run manifests record the model, forcing options and a hash of each prompt file.

Resumable on a cluster

One JSONL file per run. Re-running continues after preemption; resuming under changed settings is refused.

One command

run_all.py runs the stages in order and stops at the pilot gate unless told to continue.

Tested without a GPU

Plan

Phases and gates

1. Setup

Write load_model, pin the model revision, verify layers, experts, top-k and router names by inspection, and run the smoke test.

2. Pilot, then gate

Run H1 and H2. If H1 fails for every subject, revisit subjects, shots or model. H2a compares only the size of the routing shift, not its direction. If H2a fails, the shift is not larger than for irrelevant examples, so check H2b before concluding anything: irrelevant examples could shift routing as much but in a worse direction. If H2b fails, the shift is not specific to the examples' content, so read the forcing results with that in mind. H4 remains the causal test.

3. Main experiment

Forced routing, reverse check and sanity checks for every subject. Also replay the example tokens (graded perturbations). Done when the identity check passes, the damage anchors have run, and gain fractions with intervals exist.

4. Extensions

A second MoE model, plus the exploratory analyses on the next slide.

Plan

Possible extensions

Second MoE model

Repeat the main experiment on a different MoE architecture to test whether the split is specific to OLMoE.

Cross-domain transfer

Repeat with examples from a different subject, and plot the split against domain distance and transfer direction, using the pairs and distance ladder already built. No predicted trend. Underpowered: few pairs, a lexical distance measure, and direction confounded with target difficulty. Cross-domain gains may also be small or format-driven.

Layer-by-layer forcing

Replay routing at one layer or a window of layers instead of all of them, and sweep the layer. Shows where in the network the routing effect arises, and limits how much forced routing upstream alters the hidden state.

Hidden-state patching

Copy recorded target-token states from the other prompt at a chosen layer, with and without forced routing. Mostly informative as a layer sweep of where the gain becomes determined.

Risks

Risks

  • Forcing target tokens only still lets later layers and other positions respond; example-token replay is planned to address this.
  • Replayed routing may be out of distribution for the hidden states it meets.
  • Small per-subject question counts give wide intervals on a ratio.
  • One model may not generalize; Phase 4 adds a second.
  • Few-shot prompts are longer than zero-shot ones, so part of a gain, or of a routing shift, may reflect length and not content. The length-matched irrelevant control addresses this in the pilot, and a forced irrelevant run (planned) extends it to the forcing runs.
Discussion

Decisions needed

  • Compute: access to a GPU node for the Phase 0 check, the pilot, then the main runs.
  • Scope: which subjects, and how many? Which second MoE model for Phase 4?
  • Primary condition: is same-subject few-shot the right primary condition, with cross-domain transfer as an extension?
  • Example-token replay: Is it worth building equal-token-length example sets for matched replay?
  • Framing: is the routing-replay comparison to the merged-MoE paper enough of a contribution, or should one of the exploratory analyses (layer-wise, cross-domain transfer) be developed further?
